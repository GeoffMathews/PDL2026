Using standard candles to compare distances

Just because an object appears bright to us doesn't mean that it is actually very bright.  Imagine holding a single LED close to your eye - it could easily look brighter than every lamp in a ramp if it was close enough!  Or just because an object looks dim to us doesn't mean it isn't making very much light - think of the headlights on a car when it is miles away down the road. 

In both of these examples, if you knew what you were looking at - a single LED or a car headlight - you could use how bright it appears to estimate its distance. 

We can do the same things with astronomical objects.  If we can estimate several objects' luminosities (for example, we measure main sequence stars' spectral types and use the HR diagram to estimate how bright they are), then we can compare how bright they appear in order to determine which ones are closer or further from us.  Comparing their luminosities will tell us how would appear if they were the same distance from us.  If one appears brighter than expected, it must be closer; if it appears dimmer than expected, it must be further.
