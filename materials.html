Interpreting thermal spectra

  • Spectra
    • Python scripts for producing the following plots (code)
    • Comparison spectra of two mystery objects. Each spectrum is calculated using the Planck function, then arbitrarily scaled to create desired brightness ratio (in this case, making the hotter object dimmer, so as to confront a common misconception that brighter objects must be hotter).
    • Comparison spectra of three mystery objects, as above but allowing for multiple comparisons that will still confront the "brightness = temperature misconception" (not used in final video)
  • Photographs
    • Visible spectrum of a warm incandescent lamp (not used in final video). Diffraction grating was held over camera lens while taking the photo, so that the direct by-eye view of the lamp is on the left and the spectrum is on the right.
    • Visible spectrum of a hot incandescent lamp (not used in final video). Diffraction grating was held over camera lens while taking the photo, so that the direct by-eye view of the lamp is on the left and the spectrum is on the right.
  • Simulations
    • Spectrum constructor (screenshot used for initial graphic in video) allows user to build an arbitrary spectrum including thermal, flat, or no continuous emission, add emission and absorption lines, and Doppler shift. It presents how the object would appear by eye (the blend of colors), through a diffraction grating (which disperses the light by wavelength), and through a spectrometer. This was originally developed as part of the Foothill AstroSims project (source).
    • Stellar spectrum simulator focusing on how the temperature and size of a star determine its spectrum and luminosity (developed using Geminin, prompts for first draft, more feature-full simulation, and prompts to refine the interface).

Calculating stellar lifespans

  • Stellar lifespan calculator simulation, source (originally developed for Foothill AstroSims and revised for use in making animations, but dropped from final video). Shows a model star next to a model of the sun, on a balance that shifts to help emphasize relative mass of the two. The luminosity is also adjustable, and is represented by changing the relative brightness of the model. A Play button simulates time passing (1 billion years per second), with a Fuel Percentage displayed above the model star and the sun to help compare the relative rate of fuel usage.
  • Star graphics were edited from NASA resource "The Lives of Stars" (original)

Cepheid period-luminosity relation

  • Cepheid Property explorer simulation, source, used for screen-grab of an example light-curve. Shows how measured properties of a Cepheid star change as it pulsates, changing luminosity, size, and surface temperature over time. User can select which property (defaults to luminosity, which is the most direct observable). The simulation uses V-band magnitudes, based on the empirical model presented in Pejcha and Kochanek, 2012, as a proxy for overall luminosity, with appropriate conversation to present luminosity as a ratio to the sun's luminosity for ease of interpretation in a non-specialist environment.
  • Python Jupyter notebook for producing graphs based on Cepheid models of Pejcha and Kochanek, 2012
    • V-band period-luminosity relation (simplified labelling for use in video). Uses empirical model to calculate mean V-band magnitude by period, and converts to a brightness ratio to the sun. Labelling is simplified to avoid including distracting details.
    • lightcurve comparison for a 10 and a 25 day period Cepheid (not used in final video). Calculation of V-band brightness vs. time for two Cepheid stars, one with a period of 20 days and the other with a period of 10 days.
    • period-luminosity relation (not used in final video). Uses empirical model to calculate mean luminosity (across all wavelengths) by period, and converts to a brightness ratio to the sun. Labelling is simplified to avoid including distracting details.
    • V-band period-luminosity relation (not used in final video). Same as the first figure, but with more complete labelling (including citation to origianl data source).
  • Observations of Cepheid BASW1 (first Cepheid identified in the Andromeda galaxy)
    • g-band postage stamps downloaded from ZTF archive, selecting images from 06/02 - 08/30, 2018 (download)
    • used DS9 to manually create animation and frame-mosaic of images
    • animation of BASW1 observations, centered on BASW1 but including nearby stars for comparison. Shows June 5 through August 18, two full cycles of the star.
    • mosaic of BASW1 observations. Shows the same dates as the animated gif.
    • lightcurve file generated using ZTF catalog search, data selection, and export of data to Time-Series tool
    • Python Jupyter notebook for producing lightcurve graphs of BASW1
    • 100 day lighcurve of BASW1, in g-band, converting magnitudes to relative brightness (each 1 magnitude difference is approximately a factor of 2.5 in brightness).

Using standard candles

  • Spectrum graphic
    • Python Jupyter notebook for producing main-sequence spectral-type spectra based on sample code provided by the International Astronomical Union Office of Education (original)
    • spectra for main sequence stars from spectral type M to O, as seen by eye with a diffraction grating. Overall color gradients represent the underlying thermal spectrum (hotter things are relatively brighter at short wavelengths), with each spectrum scaled to itself (so its brightest wavelength is shown with maximum brightness). Absorption line profiles (dark bands) are simplified to just the most noticeable absorption features (H, He, Ca, Na, Fe, TiO).
  • HR diagram
    • Python Jupyter notebook for producing HR diagram based on sample code provided by the International Astronomical Union Office of Education (original)
    • HR diagram (graph of star luminosity vs. temperature) with categories and a few sample stars highlighted. Colors represent how each star would appear by-eye if we were close enough to register its color (most stars, by eye, are only bright enough to trigger our color-insensitive rods).
    • schematic HR diagram showing the main sequence, with stellar masses indicated (unused in video).

Expansion history from Hubble plots

  • Hubble plots
    • Python scripts for producting the following plots (code, datafile retrieved from Vizier Astronomical Server)
    • Blank Hubble plot, showing the apparent recession speed of a galaxy vs. its distance from us.
    • Hubble plot showing Supernova type 1A measurements from Riess et al, 2004. Catalog values for redshift and distance were converted to less technical terms, percentage of speed of light and distances in millions of light-years.
    • Hubble plot showing Supernova type 1A measurements from Riess et al, 2004, gray for overlays
    • Model Hubble plot for a simulated expansion history. This illustrates a universe that has had much more dramatic (and clearly visible by eye) changes in the expansion rate, starting with a medium, then a slow, and finally a fast expansion rate.
  • Simulations (all done with Netlogo, code is available under the Netlogo code tab for each)
    1. Cosmological Expansion, not used in review. Allows users to qualitatively explore how differences in initial separation and differences in expansion rate lead to the changing distance between a pair of galaxies.
    2. Cosmological Expansion, with graphs, not used in review. Similar to the first model, but with graphs to help track the separation and apparent velocity over time, and a third graph to compare those two dependent variables against each other.
    3. Cosmological Expansion with Multiple Galaxies, not used in review. Similar concept, but now showing how multiple galaxies appear from the perspective of an observer, with optional marks placed each billion years in the simulation to help illustrate the increase in recession velocity wih distance.
    4. Cosmological Expansion with Multiple Galaxies and Redshifting Photons, used in review. Simulation showing a number of galaxies being carried away from an observer by the epansion of space. Each galaxy emits photons towards the observer, and their relative motion and redshifting is tracked (again, taking into account the expansion of space). When each photon reaches the observer on the left, its redshift is converted to an apparent speed (via Doppler-shift equation), and its initial distance is plotted. The ability to pause and clear the plot allows for simulation of an observers' experience - i.e., we only detect and measure the photons that are reaching us now, rather than all the photons that have ever been sent to Earth - and thus, this can be used to simulate our observations of redshifts of distant objects and even qualitatively re-create (by changing the expansion rate) the observed change in the slope of the Hubble plot.

Mass of galaxy clusters from lensing

  • Simulations
    1. Gravitational lensing simulation focusing on forward modeling of lensing, rather than only tracing the paths that reach us (prompts). This simulation focuses on visualizing the multiple paths followed by light from the original source, and the role that an intervening mass can play in shifting light that was originally on a path to the observer to a different path, while causing light that would have originally missed the observer to end up reaching them, but from directions that create the illusion of the distant light source being at different locations. This simulation includes multiple tools aimed at its use for creating visualizations (adjust scaling, displaying or hiding numbers used in calculations, etc.). This simulation was built using Gemini.
    2. Original gravitational lensing simulation (used in draft video, but not in final video) tracing the paths of photons that reach us via gravitational lensing (source). Initial visualization familiarizes the user with the interface, and how the lower part of the view represents what is observed from Earth. Adding a cluster introduces gravitational lensing by that cluster, with the visualization at the bottom showing the multiple images of the distant source (though simplified to not include distortion introduced, as well). This simulation was originally developed as part of the Foothill AstroSims project, designed by Geoff Mathews and programmed by Andrew Tran.

Temperature of the universe

  • Spectra
    • Python scripts for producting the following plots (code)
    • 3000 K, visible and near-IR. Spectrum calculated using the Planck function for a 3000 K object at wvelengths from 300 to 3000 Kelvin, and color key calculated using standard conversions of wavelength to RGB color. Spectrum is scaled to show relative brightness.
    • 3000 K. Wavelengths from 200 nm to 30 mm are shown, using a log-scale (so that the progression over time can be tracked).
    • 300 K vs. 3000K
    • 30 K
    • 3 K
    • 0.3 K
    • Our CMB, vs. a cooler universe. A 2.7 K thermal spectrum is shown, as well as a 1K "mystery" spectrum, at wavelengths from 0.1 to 10 mm (log-scale).
    • Our CMB, vs. a warmer universe. A 2.7 K thermal spectrum is shown, as well as a 15K "mystery" spectrum, at wavelengths from 0.1 to 10 mm (log-scale).
  • Simulation (written in Netlogo, code is available under the Netlogo code tab)
    • Simulation of "first-light" spectra from material at a range of distances. This is an illustrative simulation of how light began to be able to travel simultaneously throughout the universe when the expansion cooled matter down to 3000 K (prior to this time, light that was emitted was almost instantly destroyed through interactions with ionized matter). It then shows how, as these first surviving spectra reach the observer, they will be increasingly redshifted (i.e., the first photons in the simulation have only been traveling for about 1 billion years, and so are only slightly redshifted. But after a much greater period of time, the arriving photons will have traveled further and thus arrive more redshifted). The ability to clear the plot allows the user to simulate the experience of a present day observer, who is not able to meausre the photons that arrived in the past or will arrive in the far future, but only those photons that are arriving right now.

Planetary habitability

  • Planetary temperature plots
    • Python scripts for producting the following plots (code)
    • baseline temperature. Solid black line showing the equilibrium temperature for a perfect absorber at distances from 0.15 to 2 AU from the sun. Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are indicated on the graph with black dots.
    • expected temperature. Same figure, but with additional blue diamonds to indicated the effective temperature based on the percentage of incoming sunlight each planet reflects.
    • surface temperature. Same figure, but with additional red squares to indicated the average surface temperature based on the percentage of outgoing infrared emissions each planet retains in its atmosphere (the greenhouse effect). Venus is off the top of the figure, and marked with an upward pointing triangle and a label.
  • Simulation
    1. Planet temperature simulator focusing on forward modeling of surface tempearture based on distance, albedo, and greenhouse effect (developed using Google Gemini, prompts). User can adjust stellar luminosity, planet distance, albedo, and greenhouse effect. The simulation also includes sonification of displayed properties.
