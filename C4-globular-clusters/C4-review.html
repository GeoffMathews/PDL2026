Calculating Stellar Lifespans

Read the following text and watch the video to review how to use spectra to compare the temperatures of dense objects.

A star finishes its formation process when it's core stabilizes - when it has been squeezed enough by gravity to reach a high enough temperature that the combined outward pressure from the gas and from fusion match the inward pull of gravity. This will continue, with the star's properties staying basically constant until the core exhausts its supply of hydrogen. We refer to the amount of time that a star stays stable, with a temperature and luminosity somewhere along the main sequence, as its main-sequence lifespan, often just abbreviated to lifespan.

The two things that determine a star's lifespan are how much fuel it has - which is set by its mass - and the rate that it is using its fuel - which is set by its luminosity. A star that is twice as massive has twice as much fuel. A star that is twice as bright is using its fuel twice as fast.

So if we have a comparison of a star's mass and luminosity with the sun's mass and luminosity, we can also compare how long it will live. More fuel shifts things to a longer life, while using that fuel faster shifts towards a shorter life. The two effects together determine the overall comparison in lifespan.
