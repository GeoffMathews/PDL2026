Using globular clusters to measure the size of our galaxy

Our galaxy includes many things like stars, gas, and dust.  While most stars are spread about orbiting on their own in the plane of the galaxy, there are some in small groups of just a few million stars called globular clusters.  These clusters of stars are distinctive because:

  • they are bright, because of the large number of stars together, and
  • since the cluster stars are closer together than most stars in the galaxy, they stand out by comparison to the scattered stars of the disk.
    •  
  • they are on orbits oriented in all directions and many of them are above or below the plane of the galaxy, so the galaxy's dust does not block our view of them.

Furthermore, every globular cluster has at least a few variable stars of types that have a strong pattern between their period of variability and their luminosity (like Cepheid and RR Lyra stars).  Since we can measure the amount of time they take to go through their brightness cycle, we can then infer their luminosity.  By comparing their luminosity to how bright they appear, we can figure out their distance.  Thus, we can use the variable stars within globular clusters to measure how far away the clusters are from us.  Placing them on a grid around us then allows us to use them to measure the size of our galaxy and find its center.   

Explore

Use the Globular Clusters 3D simulator to explore the distribution of globular clusters in a simulated galaxy, view them from different locations and angles, and compare to the topdown "map view" as seen from one location within that galaxy.

  • the initial view is how the globular clusters appear across the sky from our location within the galaxy. Moving the cursor over a globular cluster causes its distance from us to appear in a popup
  • click and drag left and right to simulate how our view of the globular clusters on the sky changes throughout the year, and we are able to see off in different directions through our galaxy (i.e., "we face different directions")
  • use the middle mouse scroll to move outward from our location or back towards it, and use the Home, Topdown, and Iso buttons to switch between different viewing locations
  • the Topdown view shows what our map of globular clusters would appear like, with the circles showing distance from us, and the angle showing the direction we have to turn and face to see each cluster
  • experiment with the properties of your galaxy - its radius, the size of the central bulge, how many globular clusters it has, our distance from the center of the galaxy. How do these change what we would see in our view from our home in this simulated galaxy? How do these change the map we would make?
